The one idea that matters

An option can lose money even when the stock moves the way you expected. This simple example shows why.

Four words to know:

  • Call: this right to buy.
  • Strike: the $17 agreed price.
  • Premium: the $2.20 paid today.
  • Break-even: $19.20, the share price needed to recover both costs.
The moral

Being right about direction is not enough. The stock must move far enough to cover what you paid for the right.

Level 01 — Contract foundations

What an option actually is

What you will learn

You will identify the buyer, seller, strike, premium, expiry and contract multiplier. You will also know what can happen when an option is exercised or assigned.

An option is a derivative — its value is derived from something else, called the underlying: a stock, an index, a commodity future.

The contract is an agreement between two people. It gives the buyer (the holder) the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell a fixed quantity of the underlying at a fixed price, at a fixed expiration date (or on and before it for American-style options). To get that right the buyer pays a non-refundable fee called the premium.

In exchange for the premium, the seller (the writer) accepts a conditional obligation. If the buyer chooses to exercise, the seller must perform (delivering shares or settling the cash difference). The buyer holds a privilege; the seller is required to honour it. That asymmetry is the whole product.

Readiness check

Can you explain who has the right, who has the obligation, what the premium buys, and how one quote becomes a contract-dollar amount? If not, reread this section before opening an option chain.

Calls, puts, and the four positions

There are two contract types, and each can be bought or sold. That is four starting positions, and every structure in Level 4 is built from them.

Call option

A call gives the holder the right to buy the underlying at the strike price on or before expiry. The seller of a call is obliged to sell at the strike if assigned.

Long call payoff at expiry P/L = max(0, S − K) − premium paid
S = stock price at expiry · K = strike

Worked example — long call

You buy one ABC call, strike $100, for a $3 premium. One contract controls 100 shares (the multiplier), so the trade costs $300. Break-even is $103.

A put gives the holder the right to sell the underlying at the strike. The seller of a put is obliged to buy at the strike if assigned.

Long put payoff at expiry P/L = max(0, K − S) − premium paid

You only ever have to learn the two long positions. Short is the same table with every sign flipped: the buyer's gain is exactly the seller's loss. Before costs, options are a zero-sum transfer between two parties.

Why options exist: insurance

Options were not invented so retail traders could gamble cheaply. They exist because investors want to transfer risk, and someone will take that risk for a fee.

You buy a Ferrari. You buy insurance on it. You pay a premium; if you crash, the insurer pays. You hope the premium is wasted money. That is exactly a put option — and the analogy is not loose, it is the mechanism.

The trade, both sides

You own 100 shares of XYZ bought at $125 — a $12,500 position that could in principle go to zero. You buy one 125-strike put for $500 as 30 days of full-coverage insurance.

Stock rises to $132
You: +$200
+$700 on the shares, −$500 on the now-worthless put. You feel ripped off. The seller keeps $500 and says thank you.
Stock falls to $113
You: −$500
The shares are down $1,200, but you exercise and sell at $125. Your only loss is the premium. The seller now owns stock $1,200 underwater.
Carry this forward

Everything in Level 06 about the variance risk premium follows from this page. Institutions are structurally afraid of crashes, so there is permanent, price-insensitive demand for puts. Insurance that people are desperate to buy tends to be sold above its fair statistical price. That gap is where professional option sellers make their living.

The other use: leverage

The same contract that hedges a portfolio also lets you control $10,000 of stock for $500. XYZ is at $100 and you think it rises.

RouteCapital outIf stock → $110ReturnIf stock stays flat
Buy 100 shares$10,000+$1,000+10%$0
Buy one 100-strike call @ $5$500+$500+100%−100%
Worked calculation

A $100-strike call bought for $5 gives you the right to buy at $100. If the stock is $110 at expiry, its value is ($110 − $100) × 100 = $1,000. The contract cost $5 × 100 = $500, so $1,000 − $500 paid = $500 profit. Break-even is $100 + $5 = $105.

Both uses are real, and the contract does not know which one you intend. What decides your outcome is not the intent but the price you paid relative to how much the stock actually moves. That is Level 2.

Non-negotiable rule

Always use limit orders on options. A market order into a wide spread is how you pay $3.00 for something worth $2.20. Even a 10¢ spread means you start the trade roughly 4% down before anything has happened.

Multiplier. Premiums are quoted per share; one US equity contract covers 100 shares. A quote of 2.33 costs $233. Mentally drop the decimal point — it becomes second nature quickly.

Contract specifications & exercise style

Every option is defined by seven things. Misunderstanding any one of them is expensive.

Underlying
The asset the contract is written on — AAPL, SPX, crude futures.
Strike
The fixed price at which you may buy (call) or sell (put).
Expiry
Last valid date. After it, the contract stops existing.
Type
Call or put.
Exercise style
American (any time) or European (expiry only).
Settlement
Physical delivery of shares, or cash for the in-the-money amount.
Multiplier
Shares per contract — almost always 100 for US equities and ETFs.

American vs European

American options allow the holder to exercise before expiration. A short writer can therefore be assigned before expiration. European options can be exercised only at expiration. Check the contract specification instead of guessing from the ticker.

OIC, “Exercising Options” says to verify the broker's exercise deadline and automatic-exercise policy. A Do Not Exercise (DNE) instruction is a broker instruction for a long option. It does not replace checking the broker cut-off or the contract terms.

Settlement

Physical settlement
Shares actually change hands. The call holder pays the strike in cash and receives shares; the put holder delivers shares and receives cash. Read the contract terms for the actual deliverable.
Cash settlement
No asset moves. The in-the-money amount is paid in cash from seller to buyer. The cash amount follows the product specification. Do not assume an index option behaves like an ETF option.

Buying versus selling. A long option has a premium paid up front. A short option creates an obligation and may require collateral. The broker's buying-power and liquidation rules are account-specific. Do not use a margin formula from a different broker as your risk number.

Exercise, assignment & expiry

Most traders close positions before expiry precisely to avoid this machinery. You still need to know how it works, because one Friday you will forget.

Opening and closing

Unlike shares, option contracts are created by trading. You buy to open or sell to open; to exit you do the opposite — sell to close or buy to close. Selling something first and buying it back later is normal here, not exotic.

Automatic exercise

Automatic exercise and broker cut-offs are contract- and broker-specific. Do not hold an option to expiry and rely on a one-cent rule. Check the broker's automatic-exercise policy and submit any DNE instruction before its stated deadline.

When a holder exercises, assignment makes a short seller fulfil the contract. The OCC assigns notices to clearing firms by its process, and each broker uses an approved method for its customers. In a multi-leg position, the broker does not automatically close every remaining leg for you. You may need to act on the long option or the delivered shares.

Early assignment: a serious account risk

You will eventually get an email titled something like “Alert: immediate action required — you have been assigned.” It is written to frighten you. What actually happened: the person on the other side exercised, and your short option became a stock position.

An oversized assigned stock position can cause a margin call, forced liquidation, broker restrictions, price loss and fees. Close before expiry or make a funded assignment plan before opening the trade. Do not assume you can calmly close and rebuild the position after assignment.

That said, early exercise is not random. There are two situations where it is the rational choice for the holder, and if you are short those contracts you should expect it:

1 · Deep ITM put — for the interest

You hold a $200-strike put with the stock at $150. Intrinsic value is $50; the option trades at $50.10, so only 10¢ of extrinsic remains. Exercising hands you $20,000 in cash today. If the interest you can earn on that cash until expiry beats 10¢, you exercise. The short seller is suddenly long 100 shares at $200.

2 · Deep ITM call — for the dividend

You hold a $90-strike call, stock at $120, trading at $30.25 — 25¢ of extrinsic. The company pays a $1 dividend tomorrow. Exercise today: give up 25¢, collect $1. The call seller is assigned and must deliver shares immediately before the dividend.

Pin risk — the real expiry danger

If the stock closes almost exactly at your short strike on expiry day, you genuinely do not know whether you will be assigned. You go home flat and arrive Monday long or short 100 shares per contract, exposed to the whole weekend. The professional answer is not to manage this — it is to eliminate it: close any short option trading near its strike in the final hours. The few cents you give up are irrelevant next to an unplanned weekend stock position. Cash-settled index options do not have this problem at all.

Beginner expiry rule

While learning, close the whole position before expiration. This removes the need to manage a last-day exercise, assignment, pin or settlement decision under time pressure.

Corporate actions

Contract terms are not permanently fixed. When a company splits its stock or pays a special dividend, the OCC adjusts open contracts so that no value is created or destroyed for either side.

Reverse split mechanics: Under standard OCC rules, the contract strike ($10) and multiplier (100) remain unchanged, but the deliverable is reduced to 20 shares. Cash required upon exercise remains Strike × Multiplier ($10 × 100 = $1,000) for 20 post-split shares, matching an effective $50/share and preserving the $1,000 notional.

Ordinary, expected cash dividends do not trigger an adjustment — they are already priced into the options. They do create the early-exercise risk described above.

A margin account is not permission to borrow

A broker may require a margin-enabled account to hold an option spread. That account label does not change a defined-risk spread into borrowed money. Keep enough cash for the planned maximum loss and fees. Do not carry a margin debit, and do not remove a protective leg so a short option becomes uncovered.

Borrowing adds interest, forced-liquidation risk and the chance of losing more than the cash set aside for the trade. This guide does not use borrowed money or uncovered short options.

The account limit is only the first gate

A loss can fit the brokerage account and still be too large for your life. Use only money that can be lost without borrowing, missing bills, using emergency savings or changing a near-term financial goal. Consider your income, debts, savings, total net worth and ability to replace a loss. There is no one percentage that suits every person.

Prefer repeatable, small, defined-risk decisions to low-probability large gains. But frequent small profits are not proof of safety. One gap or oversized loss can erase many winners.

Risk-cap checklist
  • Is the maximum loss at or below the written account cap?
  • Could I take that full loss without borrowing or harming an essential financial goal?
  • Can a gap, pin, assignment or settlement event create an exposure you cannot carry?
  • If the premium or claimed return looks unusually high, what risk is the market pricing?
  • Are fees and the bid-ask cost included in the paper record?
  • Is the winner exit, loser decision and pre-expiration close date written down?
  • Would you still open the remaining exposure today if the position moved against you?

Risk reference for a $10,000 account

Everything in this level is arithmetic. There is no judgement in it, which is why it is the part most worth getting right — you can decide it once, in advance, when you are calm.

Risk management is not an edge. It cannot turn a losing strategy into a winning one. It sets a boundary so one trade cannot decide the account.

The honest starting point

This guide uses a simple account policy. Start at 1% of account value per trade. Use 2% only as the normal maximum after the paper-trade checklist is familiar. Treat 5% as a hard ceiling, not a target. For $10,000, those amounts are $100, $200 and $500. They are derived from the account size.

How much to risk per trade

The rule, for a $10,000 account Beginner risk per position: 1%  →  $100

The ceiling is a stop on position size, not a trade target.

"Risk" here means the maximum you can lose, not what you paid. For a defined-risk spread those are different numbers, and the max loss is the one that counts.

The conclusion, stated plainly

At $10,000 with a 1–2% beginner cap, the wheel, cash-secured puts and covered calls are all mathematically out of reach. Not "risky" — out of reach. The smallest possible position is 100 shares, and on any stock durable enough to be worth owning that is $2,000–$9,000, or 20–90% of your account.

There is no strike selection, no "safe" underlying and no amount of skill that gets a cash-secured put down to $500 of risk on a quality name. The only way to shrink it is to buy a protective put underneath — at which point you have built a put credit spread, which is where this was always heading.

The good news: spreads free you from the price limit

A defined-risk spread can limit its planned loss at entry even on an expensive underlying. It can still face exercise or assignment near expiry, so close it before expiry instead of assuming the legs remove every share-delivery risk.

One thing to check on expensive stocks

Very high-priced names sometimes list strikes $5 or $10 apart near the money rather than $1 apart. If the narrowest available spread is $5 wide, your minimum risk is about $400–500 — still inside your cap, but it removes the option of going smaller. Check the strike spacing in the chain before you commit to a name.

The mistake that is not obvious: too many positions

Every source says the same thing, and it contradicts what feels sensible. More positions is not diversification.

Ten small trades each risking $250 in a $2,500 account is not spreading risk — it is risking 100% of the account at once. One source calls this "a terrible idea" in exactly those words. The reason is correlation: if you are short puts on ten stocks and the whole market falls, they do not fail independently. They fail together.

The pre-trade checklist

Written before you enter, not after it goes wrong. If you cannot answer all seven, you do not have a trade yet.

Before you click confirm
1 · The thesis
In one sentence: what do I think happens, and why is the market wrong? "It looks like it will go up" is not a thesis.
2 · Max loss in dollars
Not percent, not "defined risk" — the actual number. Is it under 1% while learning, and never above 2% as the normal maximum?
3 · Liquidity
Open interest in the thousands, volume in the hundreds. Bid-ask tight enough that a round trip does not eat the edge.
4 · Catalysts
Any earnings inside the expiry? Any ex-dividend date? Fed meeting, jobs report on the first Friday?
5 · Profit exit
The exact number at which you close. Place the good-till-cancelled order immediately after filling.
6 · Loss exit
Write the review conditions now. Do not rely only on the original credit or a percentage loss. Recheck thesis, additional risk, time, assignment risk, liquidity, and whether you would open the remaining trade today.
7 · The assignment question
If the position contains a short option, what share obligation could assignment create? For a short put, am I genuinely willing and financially able to own 100 shares at this strike? For a covered call, can I deliver the shares? For a call spread, the short call can be assigned before expiration and create short stock, share-borrow fees and margin exposure until the long leg is managed. This guide does not carry that exposure, and it closes both option legs before expiration. For the $10,000 account in this guide, a cash-secured put normally fails the ownership test.
Why write it down

The single most-cited mistake across every source is entering without an exit plan. Not bad analysis — no plan. When the trade moves against you, the decision gets made by the part of your brain least suited to making it.

Level 04 — Beginner defined-risk strategies

Structure is not strategy

This distinction separates people who collect strategy names from people who make money, and it is worth reading twice.

A structure is a combination of options — a payoff shape with a particular set of Greek exposures. An iron condor is a structure. A butterfly is a structure. That is all they are: tools.

A strategy requires an edge: a reasoned view about the future behaviour of the underlying or its volatility that differs from what the market has already priced in.

How to read the rest of this level

Each structure below gives you: the payoff shape, the Greek signature, the market thesis it expresses, the max profit / max loss / break-even formulas, and the margin reality. The combinations are effectively infinite — but every one of them decomposes into long and short single options. Understand the pieces and you can dissect anything.

Vertical spreads

Two options, same type, same expiry, different strikes. The most useful structure for a beginner, because the protective long option gives both debit and credit verticals a known maximum loss.

Long debit vertical BEGINNER · DEFINED RISK

Purpose

Use a call vertical for a limited-risk bullish view, or a put vertical for a limited-risk bearish view. It is a paper exercise first; Level 03 is the first paper-trade lesson.

Fit / avoid

Fit: you can state a directional view and accept a capped gain. Avoid: you need unlimited upside, cannot explain the view, or earnings fall before expiry.

Construction

Buy the nearer strike and sell the further strike, with the same option type and expiry. A call vertical rises with the stock; a put vertical falls with it.

Payoff

Max loss is the debit paid. Max profit is strike width minus debit. Break-even is the long strike plus debit for a call, or long put strike minus debit for a put.

Entry

Use one limit order. Check the bid-ask spread, the debit, and the full payoff before sending it. A price or DTE target is a common heuristic, not a rule.

Winner exit

Close when your written target is reached. Do not hold only because more profit is possible.

Loser exit

Close when the reason for the trade is wrong or the planned dollar loss is reached. Do not add size to repair a loss.

Time exit

Close before expiration. Time value, liquidity, and exercise choices can change the result before expiry.

Assignment

For equity and ETF options, a short leg can be assigned. Close the whole spread before expiry instead of relying on the long leg to offset it. XSP is cash-settled and has different terms.

Buy a 75 call and sell a 77 call for an $0.80 debit. One contract costs $80: 0.8 × 100 = $80. Max profit is (2.00 − 0.80) × 100 = $120. The $80 maximum loss is below the $100 learning cap.

Purpose

Fit / avoid

Construction

Sell the nearer strike and buy the further strike with the same type and expiry. Enter both legs as one defined-risk order.

Entry

The volatility point people get backwards

Common advice says buy spreads when IV is low so a rise helps you. For a vertical spread that is wrong. You want IV to fall as the stock moves your way — for debit and credit spreads alike. A rising IV only helps when the stock is moving against you, by softening the loss.

Iron condor BEGINNER · DEFINED RISK

Purpose

Use a condor only after the vertical paper exercise, when you have a range view and want both sides capped by long wings.

Fit / avoid

Fit: liquid options, no planned earnings event, and a max loss inside the account cap. Avoid: expiry day, wide spreads, or a strong directional view.

Construction

Sell one put vertical and one call vertical with the same expiry. The long put and long call are the wings.

There are two different condor studies, and they are easy to confuse. Stated separately:

  • Iron condor management, 71,417 trades: high-VIX entries had the highest profit expectancy, for both the 16-delta and 30-delta condors, after commissions. The 30-delta condor had negative expectancy in low-VIX entries.
  • Short puts, 41,600 trades: high-VIX entries combined with an active loss rule produced the best results of any bucket. Agrees.

Purpose

Protect a share position against a fall while keeping its upside.

Fit / avoid

Fit: you already own shares and accept the option cost. Avoid: using an option premium to hide a stock position that exceeds your account risk policy.

Construction

Own 100 shares and buy one put below the stock price, with the same contract multiplier.

Payoff

Below the put strike at expiry, the put limits further stock loss. The floor is a share price, not a dollar amount. The put premium is a separate cost.

Entry

Winner exit

If the shares rise, decide whether the remaining insurance still has a purpose before selling it.

Loser exit

Purpose

Reduce the cost of a protective put by agreeing to sell shares above a call strike.

Fit / avoid

Construction

Own 100 shares, buy one lower-strike put, and sell one higher-strike call with the same expiry.

Payoff

Level 05 — Execution and trade management

Choosing what to trade

A good strategy on a bad underlying loses money. Three filters decide whether a ticker belongs on your list, and the third one depends entirely on which strategy you are running.

Filter 1 · Durable
Can endure stress
A sound business or a well-built fund can still fall hard. Ask what could weaken it permanently, not whether its name feels familiar.
Filter 2 · Liquid options
You can get out
Not share volume — option volume, open interest, and a tight bid-ask. This is the filter beginners skip.
Filter 3 · Right price
Depends on strategy

The distinction that reorganises your whole list

For defined-risk spreads, share price does not set the stated maximum loss. It is set at entry. For a debit spread, it is net debit × contract multiplier. For a credit spread, it is (spread width − net credit) × contract multiplier. A 1-point-wide credit spread with the usual 100-share multiplier risks less than $100 whether the underlying trades at $30 or $800. So for spreads, prefer liquid products and use share price to check strike spacing and possible assignment exposure. A short equity or ETF option can be assigned before expiration. Holding a spread into expiration adds pin and share-delivery risk, so close the whole spread before expiration.

For the wheel, cash-secured puts and covered calls, the share price is everything, because assignment costs you strike × 100 in cash. A low share price can reduce that amount, but it does not make the position fit this guide's risk policy.

Screen the thing behind the option

Quality reduces one risk; it does not remove risk

These checks can reduce the chance of choosing a weak business. They cannot make a stock safe, stop its price falling or guarantee a profitable option trade. A strong company can become overpriced, miss expectations, lose an advantage, fall substantially and remain down for years. Position size, option price, liquidity and exit rules still matter.

The catch: one contract still controls about a tenth of the S&P's notional value, so it is a spread instrument for you, never a cash-secured put.

Section 1256 (US tax only). Index options like SPX and XSP fall under a different tax rule from stock options. Whatever you hold at year end is treated as if you sold it, and the gain is split 60% long-term and 40% short-term no matter how long you actually held it. Since short-term rates are higher, that mix is usually cheaper than typical short-term capital gains on short-duration equity options held under one year. It also sits outside the wash-sale rule. If you are not a US taxpayer this does not apply to you — check your own country's rules.

How often can you actually trade?

A days-to-expiry number does not set a fixed trade count. Your written risk limit, market conditions and exit plan set the pace.

Correction 1 · Do not let a chart choose your exit

45 DTE is a common entry example, not a holding rule. Before every exit, review your thesis, remaining maximum loss, time left, liquidity and assignment risk. A profit target or a calendar date can be part of that plan. Neither predicts how this trade will perform.

Correction 2 · You hold several positions

The limit is total risk, not a count. Keep the sum of stated maximum losses within your written account limit. The number of positions depends on their size, correlation and liquidity.

Correction 3 · You stagger the entries

Staggering entries can spread decision dates across a calendar. It is a planning choice, not a trade-frequency target.

Is there a safe high-frequency strategy for a beginner?

No. The high-frequency instruments — 0DTE and weeklies — are high-frequency because they are high-gamma. That is the same property that ends small accounts. Frequency and safety are in direct tension here, and anyone selling you both at once is selling you something.

What year one can be for

Learning to follow a written process with small, defined risk. A journal can show whether you followed your entry and exit rules. It cannot prove that a strategy will make money. Account size does not create an edge; adding capital also adds dollars at risk.

The single most repeated piece of advice across every source: do not hold a short option to expiry for the last few cents.

Very little

Everything

Two rules that sound identical and are not

"Close when 10–20% of time value remains" and "close at 80–90% of profit" land in the same place for a normal out-of-the-money trade. They do not for a put that has gone deep in the money, where intrinsic value dominates the price and time value can be almost gone while you are sitting on a large loss. Know which one you are applying.

Take-profit orders

Place a good-till-cancelled closing order at your profit target immediately after you enter. It removes the decision from the moment you are least able to make it well, and it catches spikes that happen while you are asleep.

When to close a loser

Do not use the original credit, a percentage loss, or a fixed multiple as a universal stop. Those numbers describe the past. The next decision is about the risk you still hold.

Seven questions at every review
  1. Is the original market thesis still valid?
  2. What is the maximum additional loss from this moment?
  3. What recovery is realistic, and how likely is it now?
  4. How much time remains, and how fast is gamma changing?
  5. Are assignment, dividend, borrow, pin, or settlement risks rising?
  6. Can you close near a fair price?
  7. Would you open this remaining exposure as a new trade today?

If the answer to the last question is no, close. For a beginner, closing is the normal loss decision. A rule from source material or a backtest can be a study rule for that tested setup. It is not a universal law.

The assignment playbook

Assignment is a contract outcome, not a warning signal you can time exactly. Keep short options small enough to carry, but strongly prefer closing before expiry.

Exercise and assignment

The holder of an American-style option may exercise before expiration. If you sold that option, assignment makes you fulfil the contract. A long option in the same spread does not automatically close itself or remove your delivered shares. Read the contract terms and your broker's process.

Low remaining time value can make early exercise more likely, but it is not a rule. A call holder may also consider early exercise before an ex-dividend date. You cannot infer a safe amount of time value, or a safe dollar trigger, from a screen.

Expiry, pin risk, and late notices

Pin risk means the underlying is close to a short strike near expiration. A move after the close can change whether a holder exercises. Assignment notices can arrive after normal market hours. Do not rely on a price buffer or on a broker alert arriving in time. Close short options before expiry, especially near the strike.

If you are assigned

Check the shares, cash, and any remaining options. Then follow your written plan: either hold only an exposure you can fund and still want, or close it. Your broker may restrict trading or liquidate positions under its account agreement. Do not assume a grace period or a chance to choose the order of sales.

DNE is for a long option only

If you hold a long option and do not want it exercised, ask your broker about a do-not-exercise instruction before its stated cut-off. It does not apply to an option you sold, and it does not replace checking the contract and broker policy.

Settlement choices

  • Equity and ETF options: physical settlement can create a share position when exercised or assigned.
  • XSP: Cboe specifies European exercise and cash settlement. It is one-tenth the size of SPX and has a $100 multiplier. Verify the exact series and settlement terms before trading.

Reading your account

Theory becomes trading here. Three numbers on your broker screen matter more than the rest combined.

Net liquidity · NLV
What you have
Mark-to-market value of the account if everything were liquidated at current prices. With no positions open, it is simply your cash.

What you owe the risk desk
Minimum equity required to keep your current positions open. It changes as prices move, as volatility changes, and as the broker adjusts its own risk requirements.

Roughly equity with loan value minus maintenance margin. When this gets too low or goes negative, your broker may restrict trading or liquidate positions under its account agreement.

Losses reduce NLV, which reduces excess liquidity, which shrinks your buffer — precisely when you least want it to. Getting filled without giving up your edge

You have found a mispricing. Now you have to get into it without paying away the very thing you found.

Walking the order

Place the order at the favourable end — near the ask if selling for a credit, or near the bid if buying for a debit — knowing it probably will not fill immediately. Then improve it a couple of cents at a time toward the mid until it does. You will usually get filled somewhere near the mid, not at the extreme.

Effective fill IV — the discipline that stops you chasing

Here is the critical habit. Before you start walking an order, work out what implied volatility each price actually corresponds to, and decide in advance where you stop.

The rule

Compute your effective fill IV before you walk the order, and set a hard stop. A fill is not a success if the price you accepted destroyed the reason for the trade. No fill is strictly better than a bad fill.

Expect to be down at entry

Almost every option spread shows a negative unrealised P/L the moment it fills. That is not a mistake — options have wide spreads and the mark price sits near the mid, so by the time you get filled your position is marked slightly against you. Normal. Do not react to it.

Screening for a trade

Three features are empirically predictive of variance-risk-premium returns. Tested across stocks and ETFs above $10, with at least 25,000 contracts of 20-day average option volume, excluding earnings events:

1 · IV / RV ratio

The higher current IV sits above trailing RV, the better the average 30-DTE straddle return. This confirms that backward-looking RV is a reasonable baseline proxy for future RV.

Want: IV comfortably above recent RV.

2 · IV percentile

Counter-intuitively, the lower the one-year IV percentile, the better the average short-vol return. Very high IV percentiles often mean something genuinely bad is happening.

Want: IV not at an extreme high versus its own history.

3 · Term structure slope

The more positive the slope — that is, the deeper the contango — the better the average return. Backwardation warns of stress or a known imminent event.

Want: a clean upward-sloping term structure.

These are generalities, not a checklist

A screen produces candidates, not trades. You still assess each situation on its own: what is driving the IV, is there an event, how liquid is the chain, what does the skew do to the structure you plan to use. The screen tells you where to look.

Position sizing: why the maths says a number you must ignore

Your working rule was set in Level 02 — 1–2% per trade, 5% ceiling. This section explains where that kind of number comes from, and why the textbook answer is far too large to use.

The Kelly criterion gives the bet size that maximises long-run terminal wealth. Optimisers will happily tell you Kelly is 25–30% of your account on a given trade. Do not do that.

Why full Kelly is unusable

Kelly only cares about the final value. It is completely indifferent to the path. Betting full Kelly produces portfolio variance and 50%+ drawdowns that are psychologically unbearable and practically unsustainable — you will stop trading the strategy long before the maths pays off.

Why the number itself is too big

These simulations typically use geometric Brownian motion, which assumes normally distributed returns. Real distributions have fat tails and negative skew. The model understates how far and how fast prices can move against you, so its Kelly output is optimistic by construction.

Practical sizing Use 5–10% of the calculated Kelly fraction, or less.

Example: Kelly says 30%. Take 5% of that → 1.5% of account value in premium sold.
On the $110,000 account in that example, it was about $1,700 of premium, or 4 contracts. Scaled to your $10,000 it is about $150 — which in practice means one contract of a narrow defined-risk spread, not four of anything.
Do not stack the same view twice

In the worked example below, four lots of a straddle and four lots of a condor were opened on the same underlying for illustration. That is the same short-volatility position at double size. In real trading you take one or the other. Two structures expressing one thesis is not diversification.

Exits, judged on the thesis rather than the P/L

Level 05 gives a forward-looking review earlier in this level. Do not keep a loser only because of its original credit or percentage loss. This section adds exit conditions that fire because the reason for the trade has changed.

Decide your exit conditions before you enter. For a short-volatility trade there are three, and two of them are about the thesis rather than the P/L.

A trade is not judged by its outcome

Just because a trade did not make money does not mean it was a bad trade — and just because it did does not mean it was a good one. You control the process: the forecast, the structure, the fill, the size, the exit rule. The outcome of any single trade is dominated by randomness. Judge the process; let the sample judge the outcomes.

Level 06 — Greeks, price and volatility

Intrinsic vs extrinsic value

The premium is not one number. It is always the sum of two very different things, and understanding every strategy in this guide hangs on telling them apart.

The only decomposition you need Premium = intrinsic value + extrinsic value

Intrinsiccall = max(0, S − K)    Intrinsicput = max(0, K − S)
Extrinsic = Premium − Intrinsic

Intrinsic value is the tangible part: what the option would be worth if exercised right now. It is the amount by which the option is in the money. It can never be negative, because you can always decline to exercise.

Extrinsic value is everything else. It is often called time value, which is slightly wrong — it is driven by two forces, time remaining and implied volatility. It is the price of the possibility that the option becomes profitable, or more profitable, before it expires. It is the value of uncertainty, and it is what a seller collects.

Worked example

Stock ABC at $105, 100-strike call trading at $6.50. Intrinsic is $105 − $100 = $5.00. Extrinsic is $6.50 − $5.00 = $1.50. That $1.50 is what the buyer pays for the remaining time and the chance the stock climbs further; it is what the seller collects for carrying the risk that it does.

At expiry
Extrinsic = 0
No time left, no uncertainty. Every option is worth exactly its intrinsic value. This is why an out-of-the-money option becomes worthless.
At the money
Extrinsic is maximal
Maximum uncertainty about the final outcome, so the highest price for that uncertainty — all else equal.
Out of the money
100% extrinsic
No intrinsic value at all. The entire premium is a bet on future movement.
The asymmetry that defines the game

When you buy an option you are paying for extrinsic value, and extrinsic value is a decaying asset. When you sell one, you collect that decaying value up front. This is why many option sellers favour the sell side. It is not because selling is free money. The chapters on Greeks, volatility and edge in this level explain why.

Time decay in practice

Options have expiry dates for the same reason car insurance is billed monthly. You do not pay once and stay covered forever. Six months of cover costs about $600; twelve months costs about $1,200. An option with 60 days to expiry costs more than the same option with 30 days.

But the relationship is not linear — and this is the first genuinely quantitative idea in the guide. Volatility scales with the square root of time, so a 60-day option is not twice the price of a 30-day option. All else equal it is about √2 ≈ 1.41×. We come back to this properly in Level 06.

Why an out-of-the-money option has any value at all

If every OTM option is worthless at expiry, why does the market pay for one today? Because the stock still has time to move. A 20-strike call on an $18 stock with 28 days left has value; the same call with zero days left is worth nothing. The value is the remaining chance.

That is also why the further out of the money you go, the cheaper it gets: an $18 stock is much more likely to reach $20 in 28 days than to reach $25.

The break-even that catches everyone

Twitter at $18.09. The 17-strike call with 28 days costs $2.20 — that is $1.09 intrinsic plus $1.11 extrinsic. To break even at expiry the stock must reach $17 + $2.20 = $19.20, a 6% move in four weeks, just to get your money back. Buy the option and hold it flat for 28 days and you lose $111 with the stock unchanged.

Volatility: the third driver

Most option traders learn the first two drivers, ignore this one, and then wonder why they cannot make money.

Volatility is the magnitude of a stock's price swings. Technically it is the annualised standard deviation of logarithmic returns. Critically, it is directionless — it measures how much, not which way.

Why higher volatility means higher premiums

Go back to the insurance frame. You are an insurer writing life cover for two people: one overweight, smoking a pack a day, with a serious condition; one healthy and active. Who pays more? Obviously the riskier one. A high-volatility stock is the first person.

The mathematical version is more interesting. Volatility widens the whole distribution of possible future prices — symmetrically, in both directions. But an option only collects from one side of that distribution:

The put works identically in reverse: it only cares about the left tail, and the extra upside the wider distribution creates does nothing for it. So volatility does not raise option prices because it has a bullish or bearish opinion. It raises them because it expands the range of outcomes, and the option only counts the side where it can pay.

Volatility as synthetic time

Here is a mental model worth keeping. A high-volatility stock with one month to expiry can have almost the same range of possible outcomes as a low-volatility stock with six months. Increasing either time or volatility widens the terminal distribution in a very similar way.

Treat them as two sides of one quantity — total uncertainty. This is why gamma and theta are inseparably linked in the Greeks chapter, and why the √time rule ties vega to theta in the volatility chapter of this level.

Black–Scholes, honestly

You do not need the derivation. You need to know what the model does, what it assumes, and where those assumptions break — because the breakages are where the money is.

Fischer Black, Myron Scholes and Robert Merton published in 1973, the same year the CBOE opened. The basic formula values a European option on a non-dividend-paying asset. Its inputs are spot, strike, time, rate and volatility. The usual Black–Scholes–Merton version also includes a known dividend yield or the present value of expected cash dividends.

Where the model is wrong

Black–Scholes is famously described as “wrong, but useful”. Each of its assumptions fails in a specific, tradable way:

The map is not the territory

The model does not tell you what an option should cost. Its real job is to give you a standard language for asking intelligent questions: is the market's implied volatility a fair price for the volatility I expect this stock to actually realise? Why are downside puts priced at a higher IV than upside calls, and is that skew steep or flat versus its own history? The edge is in answering those. The model only lets you ask them.

What implied volatility really is

This is the most-misunderstood concept in retail options, and it is genuinely simple once stated the right way round.

In modern practice nobody uses Black–Scholes to work out what an option should cost. The market sets the price through supply and demand. Traders take the known market price and run the model backwards to solve for the one variable they cannot observe: volatility. The answer is the implied volatility.

Why bother

Raw premiums are not comparable. Is an $8 at-the-money call on a $400 stock more expensive than a $5 at-the-money call on an $80 stock? In dollars, yes. In any meaningful trading sense, the $5 option is dramatically more expensive — it is 6.25% of the underlying versus 2%.

Converting every premium to implied volatility compresses all that away into one standardised figure. A trader can then say: “this market is pricing 25% annualised uncertainty” — and compare it across names, across expiries, and against its own history. A professional will almost always quote an option in IV terms, not dollars.

The rule of 16

There are about 252 trading days in a year, and √252 ≈ 15.87. So dividing an annualised IV by 16 converts it into an expected daily move. This is the single most useful piece of mental arithmetic in options.

The fear gauge

What moves IV? Supply and demand for the options themselves. Lots of buyers → options get dearer → IV rises. And what creates lots of option buyers? Fear. When investors feel exposed, they buy protection. That is why implied volatility can behave as a market fear gauge. It is not the IV of one at-the-money option, and it does not describe an individual stock.

What "sensitivity" means

A derivative measures a rate of change. Your car is at mile marker 100 — that is your state. Your speedometer reads 60 mph — that is the first derivative, position with respect to time. Press the accelerator and your speed changes: acceleration is the derivative of the derivative.

Every Greek is the same question: if this input moves a little, how much does the option price move? Theta is price with respect to time. Vega is price with respect to volatility. That is all the calculus you need.

Delta — directional exposure

Delta is the change in option price for a $1 change in the underlying. A delta of 0.60 means the option gains 60¢ if the stock gains $1.

Calls: 0 → +1 Puts: 0 → −1 At the money ≈ ±0.50

Three practical uses

1 · Equivalent stock position

A portfolio with total delta +150 behaves — for small moves — like owning 150 shares. Delta −25 is like being short 25 shares. This translates a basket of options into one number you can reason about.

2 · Hedge ratio

Stock has a delta of exactly 1. A small, immediate move in a position with delta −75 can be offset by buying about 75 shares. Delta then changes with price, time and volatility, so this is only the first hedge, not a lasting neutral position. See delta hedging.

3 · Rough probability

Not mathematically identical, but traders use delta as a back-of-envelope probability of finishing in the money. A 0.12-delta call ≈ 12% chance of finishing ITM, so ≈ 88% chance of expiring worthless — which is precisely how option sellers pick strikes.

Delta is not static

More time or higher volatility widens the distribution of possible outcomes, and that pushes all deltas toward 0.50:

  • Out-of-the-money options: a wider distribution is pure upside — more chance of travelling far enough to finish ITM. Delta rises from near 0 toward 0.50.
  • In-the-money options: a wider distribution introduces doubt — more chance of falling back out. Delta drops from near 1 toward 0.50.
  • At-the-money options: the two effects cancel. Delta sits around 0.50.

Gamma — the engine of optionality

Gamma is the rate of change of delta: how much your delta moves for a $1 change in the underlying. It is the second derivative, and it is what makes an option an option rather than a stock position.

If a call has delta 0.50 and gamma 0.10, a $1 rise takes delta to about 0.60. Gamma is positive for every long option and negative for every short one.

Long gamma — you own options
Favourable
Your exposure accelerates in your favour and decelerates against you. Move your way → delta grows → gains magnify. Move against you → delta shrinks → losses slow. You pay for this through theta.
Short gamma — you sold options
Unfavourable
Exactly reversed. Move against you → delta becomes more adverse → losses accelerate. Move your way → delta shrinks → gains slow. You are paid for this through theta.

Two nuances worth knowing

  • Gamma is inversely proportional to the stock price. An option on a $50 stock has higher gamma than the same option on a $200 stock, because $1 is a bigger percentage move for the cheaper stock. Vega and theta go the other way — they are larger in dollar terms on higher-priced underlyings.
  • For ATM options, gamma rises as volatility falls. Low volatility or little time forces the option into a binary decision, which is exactly when delta is most unstable. For clearly ITM or OTM options the relationship reverses.

Theta — and why it is not an edge

Theta is the expected loss of value per day passed, all else equal. A theta of −0.05 means the option loses 5¢ a day.

The relationship that matters more than the definition Theta is the rent you pay to be long gamma,
or the rent you collect for letting someone else be long gamma.

Within the Black–Scholes framework, gamma and theta are inseparable. You cannot have one without the other. Long an option → positive gamma (good) → negative theta (you pay). Short an option → negative gamma (bad) → positive theta (you are paid). Time is your enemy or your friend depending entirely on which side of that trade you took.

The most expensive misunderstanding in retail options

Enormous numbers of traders believe they have an edge because they systematically sell options “to collect theta.” They do not. Theta is not a source of profit. If options were priced perfectly — implied volatility exactly equal to the volatility that later occurs — then buying and selling would both have zero expected value, regardless of who is collecting theta.

What premium sellers are actually doing is making a forecast: that future realised volatility will come in below the implied volatility priced in today. When that works, they are harvesting the variance risk premium — a real, documented phenomenon. The edge lives in the volatility forecast, not in the time decay. Confusing the two is what gets accounts destroyed.

When theta is largest

  • At the money — most extrinsic value to lose.
  • Close to expiry — decay accelerates sharply in the final days.
  • Higher-priced underlyings — bigger premiums mean more absolute value to decay.
  • Higher implied volatility — more extrinsic value at stake to erode.

Notice the pairing: as expiry nears, gamma spikes for ATM options — and theta spikes to match. The rising cost of gamma exposure is exactly offset by accelerated decay. That is not a coincidence; it is the mechanism.

Vega — exposure to the volatility forecast

Vega is the change in option price for a one percentage point change in implied volatility. Vega 0.15 means the option gains 15¢ if IV goes from 25% to 26%.

Long options: positive vega Short options: negative vega Highest: at the money, long dated

Vega is not a real Greek letter (it is a financial pseudo-Greek), and while classic Black–Scholes assumes volatility is constant over time, vega is formally obtained as the partial derivative of option price with respect to the implied volatility parameter (∂V/∂σ). In practice it is arguably the most important Greek for a professional, because it is the direct measure of exposure to the thing you are actually trying to forecast.

The strategic split

Gamma is highest for short-dated options. Vega is highest for long-dated ones. That single asymmetry decides your expiry choice:

  • Near-term options are gamma instruments. You are trading the path — how much the stock actually jiggles day to day.
  • Long-dated options are vega instruments. You are trading the market's opinion about volatility itself.

Rho & the higher orders

Rho is sensitivity to a 1-point change in the risk-free rate. Higher rates raise call values and lower put values — the logic runs through the carrying cost of the replicating portfolio. For most short-dated strategies rho is negligible. It matters for long-dated options and in periods of rapid rate policy change. For practical purposes, it is the least important Greek.

One model that explains all of them

Do not memorise the rules. Every Greek behaviour falls out of one picture: the probability distribution of the stock price at expiry.

At the money = maximum uncertainty

For any given expiry, gamma, theta and vega are all highest at the money. It is the inflection point — the option will resolve either into or out of the money, and it is most sensitive to changes in price, time and volatility right there.

Certainty kills the Greeks

Far out of the money, an option can be close to worthless. Deep in the money, it can behave much like the underlying. Gamma, theta and vega may be small at either extreme, while delta approaches 0 for far OTM options and ±1 for deep ITM options.

The effect of time is twofold

As expiry approaches the future becomes more certain and the range of outcomes narrows — that decreases vega. But that same certainty forces a binary outcome for at-the-money options, which makes gamma and theta skyrocket. Same cause, opposite effects, and this is exactly the near-term/long-term split above.

Where the Greeks stop working

In the final hours of an at-the-money option's life, the smooth world the Greeks describe breaks down entirely. Gamma approaches infinity; delta swings violently between 0 and 1 on the smallest tick, making traditional delta hedging both impossible and ruinously expensive. Theta explodes as the last time value evaporates.

Risk management has to shift from continuous to binary. The question stops being “what is my delta?” and becomes “will this be exercised or not?” The dominant risk is pin risk and an unwanted overnight stock position — not the hedging of a delta that has become meaningless.

The square-root-of-time rule

Volatility is often annualised with √t. This is a useful approximation, not a law of markets. It assumes independent returns with stable variance and can fail around jumps, events, changing volatility and non-trading periods.

The drunkard's walk

A drunk leaves a bar. Each step is random — some left, some right. After four steps, is he four units from the door? Almost certainly not, because the steps cancel. The root-mean-square (RMS) displacement grows with the square root of the number of steps (σ ∝ √n): four steps → 2 units RMS, 100 steps → 10 units RMS (the expected absolute distance scales as √(2/π)√n ≈ 0.8√n).

Why √t Variance is additive over time:   Var(t) = t · σ²
Volatility is the square root of variance:   σ(t) = σ · √t

Annualising: σannual = σdaily · √252 = σweekly · √52

Black–Scholes uses this scaling under its assumptions. It is useful for annualising an estimator, but it is not a precise link between an option's vega and theta or a price forecast.

The rule of 16, again

√252 ≈ 15.87 ≈ 16. Annualised IV ÷ 16 is a one-standard-deviation daily scale under the same approximation: IV 32% gives about 2%, and IV 80% about 5%. It is not a daily price target or a promise that moves stay inside that range.

The variance risk premium

Compare implied volatility to the volatility that later occurred, across thousands of observations, and one fact dominates everything else: on average, IV is higher than the RV that follows.

That systematic overpricing is the variance risk premium — the VRP — and it is the closest thing to a durable, repeatable edge available in the options market.

Why it exists

Not a market inefficiency. A risk premium — the fair, rational price for carrying a risk other people are desperate to offload.

Why sellers demand a premium

Being short volatility is structurally scarier than being long stock, in three specific ways:

  • Convex losses. Own a stock and it falls $1, you lose $1 — linear, easy to reason about. Short a straddle and it falls $1, you lose some amount; as it keeps falling your delta grows more negative through negative gamma, so your rate of loss increases. Losses accelerate exactly when you can least afford it.
  • Unbounded tail. A stock's loss is capped at zero. A naked short call has no ceiling at all.
  • Gap vulnerability. A long stock position survives an overnight gap. A dynamically hedged short-vol position cannot be hedged through one, and can take a catastrophic, unmanageable loss in an instant.

Because most participants want nothing to do with that profile, the price of options is pushed systematically upward. The mirror image is also true: option buyers are willing to systematically overpay for the very attractive positive-gamma profile where losses decelerate and gains accelerate.

Do not over-generalise this

The VRP is a tendency, not a law. It is not present on every underlying, and it is not available at all times. Level 05 covers how to actually test for it on a specific name before trading it. "Sell premium, it works on average" is how people discover the average includes 2008.

Measuring realized volatility

To judge whether IV is rich you need a benchmark, and the benchmark is your best estimate of realized volatility. Measuring it well is a science in itself.

Always use log returns

Professionals use ln(Pt / Pt−1), never simple percentage change. Two reasons, both structural:

Log returns are additive

A stock starts at $100 and returns +10%, −5%, +8%. The simple returns do not sum to 13% — you have to chain the multiplications, and the true answer is 12.86%.

Log returns just add: 0.0953 − 0.0513 + 0.0770 = 0.1210. Then $100 × e0.1210 = $112.86. Identical answer, trivially composable across periods.

Log returns are more stationary

Statistical models need data whose properties do not drift. Raw prices are non-stationary — and a $1 move means 10% on a $10 stock but 2% on a $500 stock.

Log returns are effectively unitless percentage changes, so you can compare a stock's volatility across price regimes and long histories without the numbers lying to you.

RV is an estimate, not a fact

Think of price movements as coming from a machine with a true internal volatility setting. You never get to read that setting. You only see the output — a series of prices — and you reverse-engineer your best guess. Because the sample is limited, the guess carries statistical error, and your choices about which data to use change the answer significantly.

LookbackStrengthWeaknessFailure mode
Long — 180 to 252 days Large sample, stable estimate Contaminated by old regimes Early 2022: a 252-day RV still includes months of zero-rate 2021 calm, so it reads far too low for a market that has just changed character
Short — 20 to 30 days Responsive to current conditions Small sample, easily distorted One 15% gap on a normally-quiet stock makes the 20-day RV scream "highly volatile" when it was really a single shock

And what do you do with a genuine one-off — a biotech that fell 40% on a failed trial inside your 30-day window? There is no single right answer, and quant firms apply proprietary rules:

  • Include it: it happened. It is a real part of the return distribution, and removing it makes you underestimate the tail risk you are selling.
  • Cap or exclude it: if you want to know typical day-to-day volatility, that one day pollutes the next 30 days of estimates. A firm might cap any single return at four or five standard deviations.

The practical answer: compute RV over several lookbacks — 10, 30 and 90 days — with different event-handling rules, and look at the picture rather than a number.

How volatility behaves

Volatility is not a random number. It has consistent, observable habits across almost every market. Knowing them is how you build intuition for what is normal — and notice when something is not.

1 · It is directionless

A stock can have identical volatility while trending up, trending down, or chopping sideways. The paths and outcomes differ completely; the magnitude of the daily fluctuations is the same. Never read "high volatility" as "the market is going down."

2 · But it correlates with direction — spot-vol

In equities, when the market falls, volatility rises sharply. When it rises, volatility drifts lower. This inverse relationship is the most dominant fact in equity options, and it is why the VIX is called the fear gauge.

This is not universal

Gold often has near-zero spot-vol correlation — as a safe haven its volatility can rise in both up and down markets. Oil and commodities can be positively correlated, because rising prices often come with geopolitical tension and supply uncertainty. Applying an equity mindset to a commodity option is an expensive mistake.

3 · Volatility is itself volatile

"Vol of vol." When market volatility is high, its day-to-day swings are also larger. The VVIX — the implied volatility of the VIX — is high when VIX is at 40 and low when VIX is at 12. In a high volatility environment the risk is not just that the market moves a lot; it is that your measure of risk is also unstable.

4 · It clusters

Volatility is autocorrelated: today's level is a good predictor of tomorrow's. On any price chart you see distinct periods of turbulence and calm, not evenly scattered spikes. For a trader, this means your best short-term estimate of tomorrow's volatility is today's volatility.

5 · And it mean-reverts

Over longer horizons, volatility returns to a long-run average. A VIX of 50 after a crash will not stay at 50 — acute fear resolves. A VIX below 10 reflects complacency and an underpricing of risk, and will not last either.

The volatility surface

If Black–Scholes were correct, every option on a given stock would imply the same volatility, and a plot of IV across strikes and expiries would be a flat plane.

It is not. It is a three-dimensional surface with slopes and valleys, and understanding its shape matters enormously — because the surface is the market. Its shape tells you the real price of risk for different outcomes and time horizons.

Why does the surface exist? Partly because the model's assumptions are flawed — but mostly because of supply and demand. The price of any specific option is set by how many people want to buy it versus sell it. The surface is the market's collective risk appetite made visible.

Skew

Take one expiry and plot IV against strike. In equities you almost never get a symmetric smile. You get a downward-sloping smirk: out-of-the-money puts imply a lot higher volatility than at-the-money options, which in turn imply more than out-of-the-money calls.

What the shape is telling you

The skew is the market's implied view of the distribution of future returns, and it says two things a normal bell curve does not:

Negative skewness
A long left tail
Puts at much higher IV than ATM means the market is putting extra weight on sharp downside moves. Traders will pay more for crash protection than for equivalent upside exposure. The implied distribution is tilted, not balanced.
Leptokurtosis
Fat tails
Both wings priced above the middle means the market expects extreme moves far more often than a normal distribution implies. Options are not priced as if the world is smooth and symmetric.

The two forces that carve it

  • Downside skew premium — fear. Persistent institutional demand for OTM puts as portfolio insurance. To be coaxed into selling crash protection, sellers demand a premium not just for the probability of a crash but for the convexity of that risk — the fact that losses accelerate catastrophically in a downturn. This is what makes the smirk steep.
  • Upside skew premium — greed. On some names, consistent demand from speculators treating far OTM calls as lottery tickets. They overpay for a small chance at a huge payoff, and sellers charge for the unlimited-loss exposure. This is what makes the far right tail flatten or tick up. It is pronounced on high-retail-interest names.
Skew matters more often than you think

A common mistake is to assume skew only matters when you set out to trade skew. In reality, the moment your position uses more than one strike, skew is part of the trade. Buy one strike and sell another and part of your P/L is the relationship between their volatilities. It is not usually the dominant term — delta and the stock's move usually matter more — but it affects what you pay, what you collect, and how the position behaves.

A concrete case from Level 05: on GME, buying the protective wings of an iron condor meant buying a much higher implied volatility than you were selling at the centre. The skew ate a large slice of the trade's theoretical edge before it even started.

Term structure & forward volatility

Now take the at-the-money options across every expiry and plot IV against time. That is the term structure, and its shape is one of the most powerful sentiment indicators available.

Contango — the normal state

Long-term IV > short-term IV. Logical: over a longer horizon there are more economic reports, more geopolitical events, more company news that could happen. The market charges more to insure against a greater number of unknown unknowns. An upward-sloping term structure is one of the better predictors of short-volatility returns.

Backwardation — the stress signal

Short-term IV > long-term IV. Two causes: panic now — a crisis or crash driving a rush for immediate protection; or known event risk — an earnings report next week inflating the weekly that expires just after it, while later expiries are barely affected. It is an inherently unstable state and usually reverts to contango, often alongside a sharp drop in overall volatility.

Forward volatility

Because variance adds over time, the term structure lets you back out the market's price for volatility over a specific future window. If you know 30-day and 60-day IV, you can solve for the 30-day period beginning 30 days from now.

Forward volatility between T₁ and T₂ σfwd = √( ( σ₂²·T₂ − σ₁²·T₁ ) / ( T₂ − T₁ ) )

Example: 30-day IV = 22%, 60-day IV = 28% → 30-to-60-day forward vol ≈ 32.9%

That number is tradable. Think of the 60-day option as containing two pieces: the first 30 days (which you already know from the 30-day option) and the next 30 days — the forward window. A calendar spread — same strike, same type, different expiries — is a bet on that forward window. The near-period volatility largely cancels out.

  • Long calendar (sell the near, buy the far): you are buying forward volatility.
  • Short / reverse calendar (buy the near, sell the far): you are selling forward volatility.

Delta hedging

Having a volatility view is not enough. You have to isolate it from the noise of daily price direction — otherwise the direction dominates your P/L and drowns out the thing you were actually right about.

The tool is delta hedging: systematically buying and selling the underlying so your net delta stays at or near zero. The purpose is to convert a position sensitive to direction into one sensitive to magnitude — that is, to volatility.

Advanced reference: variance and path, not a P/L identity A delta-hedged option result depends on realised variance versus the implied variance paid, the whole price path, changing gamma and vega, jumps, skew, rates, dividends and every hedge cost.

Lower realised volatility than IV does not guarantee a profit. A jump or frequent costly hedges can still produce a loss.

Market efficiency

You now understand the instrument and you understand volatility. That still leaves the only question that decides whether you make money: why should any trade you place have positive expected value at all?

Start with the uncomfortable truth. Most aspiring traders lose money, and it is not for lack of intelligence or effort. It is because they underestimate the obstacle they face every day: markets are mostly efficient.

The efficient market hypothesis says prices rapidly incorporate all available information. In a perfectly efficient market there are no bargains and no overpriced assets, and the expected profit of any trade is zero before costs. Eugene Fama, who built the theory, was the first to say perfect efficiency is a benchmark rather than a description of reality — and the gaps are where you live.

The three forms, and where each one leaks

FormClaimsImpliesReality
Weak All past price and volume data is in the price Technical analysis cannot consistently produce an edge — any real pattern would be arbitraged away instantly by algorithms Mostly true, but not absolute. Momentum persists: big winners tend to keep winning, big losers to keep losing
Semi-strong All public information is in the price Reading a positive story in the Wall Street Journal and buying should give you nothing — the price adjusted before you finished the headline Largely true for large caps. Post-earnings drift shows convergence is not instant
Strong All information, including private, is in the price Even inside information gives no edge clearly false. The existence of insider-trading law is the proof

Where the frictions are

  • Costs. Perfect efficiency assumes zero transaction costs and free instant information. Every trade costs commission and, more importantly, spread. Good information and the tools to analyse it are expensive. Any edge must clear that bar first.
  • Human beings. Herding creates momentum, bubbles and crashes that overshoot fundamental value. The disposition effect — selling winners too early and holding losers too long — is well documented. Traders over-react to dramatic news.
  • Convergence takes time. Even when something is mispriced, it does not snap back instantly. The path of convergence is where the opportunity lives — and where the risk is.
Proof that markets are not perfectly efficient

In early 2020, as Zoom Video Communications (ticker ZM) became a household name, a tiny unrelated company called Zoom Technologies (ticker ZOOM) rose over 1,800%. Sometimes the market cannot process even the most basic public information correctly.

The working assumption: treat the market as highly efficient by default. Respect it. Expect no easy money. But know that it is not perfectly efficient, and your job is to hunt in the gaps left by behavioural biases, structural frictions and information costs.

Adverse selection — getting filled is information

A jar holds an unknown number of coins. You offer $20. The seller instantly agrees. Should you feel clever, or worried?

You should be worried. Their instant acceptance is information: they probably know it holds $15.

This reframes what trading is. Every trade is an assertion that the market's price is wrong relative to a fair value you have calculated. You are not betting on a company's success; you are betting on the mispricing of risk. And you are doing it in a zero-sum environment — after costs, negative-sum — where every dollar you make is taken from someone trying just as hard to take it from you.

Meanwhile the other side may be a billion-dollar fund with microwave towers for millisecond advantages and PhD mathematicians modelling every conceivable correlation. Your dedication is not an edge.

Expected value

Expected value is the average outcome of a bet if repeated infinitely — the probability-weighted sum of every outcome.

Expected value EV = (Pwin × amount won) − (Ploss × amount lost)

EV is a long-run average, not a prediction about the next trade. A positive-EV trade can lose. A negative-EV trade can win. The goal is not to win every trade — it is to place trades where the odds favour you and let the law of large numbers work.

Win rate, on its own, means nothing

Strategy A
Wins 2 of 10
If the two winners are big enough to more than cover eight losses, this is a positive-EV strategy — and a perfectly good business.
Strategy B
Wins 95 of 100
If the five losers are large enough to wipe the account, this is negative EV. It feels safe and consistent for a very long time, and then it is not.

This is exactly why high win-rate strategies are so seductive and so dangerous. Selling premium is a high win-rate strategy. That is not the reason it works — and if you think it is, the day the loss arrives will be instructive.

Drawdown is the normal state

Even with a real edge, you will spend most of your time in a drawdown, not at new highs. Equity curves make new highs only occasionally; most of the journey is spent recovering or moving sideways. A drawdown is not evidence that something is broken. It is the shape of a probabilistic edge.

Risk management is not an edge

This is the most dangerous misconception among developing traders, and the cleanest way to demolish it is with a roulette wheel.

An American roulette wheel has 18 red, 18 black and 2 green — 38 slots. You bet $100 on red.

Expected value of a $100 bet on red P(win) = 18/38 = 47.37%  ·  P(lose) = 20/38 = 52.63%
EV = (0.4737 × $100) − (0.5263 × $100) = −$5.26

Now be disciplined about it. Start with $10,000. Risk no more than 1% per spin — $100. Stop for the day after a 5% drawdown or five consecutive losses. It sounds professional. It will certainly stop you losing everything in one reckless evening.

And it does nothing to your expected value. Every spin is still −$5.26. All the plan changed is the path of your losses. Instead of losing the money quickly, you now lose it slowly and methodically, one disciplined $100 bet at a time.

What an edge actually requires

  • You must define it before risking a dollar. If you cannot explain your edge in two sentences to a child, you probably do not have one. Why is the market paying you? What value are you providing?
  • You must quantify it. Backtests and data are what turn a feeling into a boundary. Quantification removes emotion, tells you your worst realistic case, and gives you a sense of the P/L paths your account can take — so you are not fearful in drawdowns or greedy in good runs.
  • You must try to disprove it. A professional trader is a scientist attacking their own hypothesis. If the data does not support the edge, the strategy is discarded.
  • Trading for excitement is a path to ruin. Good trading is boring. Placing and managing the trade is usually the smallest part of the work.

Edges decay

Markets are adaptive systems. Find a signal that says an option is 5% underpriced and, as more traders use it, they all try to buy — the ask rises until the mispricing is gone. Often the quotes move before a single trade prints, because the market-making algorithms learned the signal too.

A strategy that worked in 2022 carries no guarantee for 2026. Survival requires continuous refinement, hunting for new niches, and adapting. If you are not learning and improving, others are — which means you are falling behind.

The two risk premia that matter to you

Equity risk premium

The excess return of stocks over the risk-free rate. Buy a bond and you are a lender with a contractual claim, near the front of the queue in bankruptcy. Buy a stock and you are an owner with a residual claim on uncertain profits, paid dead last — usually nothing. No rational investor takes that risk for the same expected return as a treasury bill. The demand for compensation is the premium. It is not an inefficiency; it is the fair price of bearing the risk of capitalism.

Variance risk premium

Just as stocks must pay a premium over bonds, selling options typically pays a premium over simply owning stocks — because the risk profile is worse in the specific ways covered in Level 06: convex accelerating losses, an unbounded tail, and vulnerability to gaps. The VRP is the compensation for taking on exactly the risk everyone else is paying to avoid.

Where inefficiencies actually appear in options

  • Post-earnings announcement drift — stocks with large positive surprises tend to drift higher for days or weeks as the market digests the news. Convergence is not instant.
  • Index rebalancing — when a stock joins a major index, index funds are forced to buy, creating predictable pressure.
  • Mispriced volatility in single names — the richest ground for an options trader. Broad indices are highly efficient; individual stocks outside the mega-caps are not. Information disseminates more slowly, a single large participant can distort a thin chain, and fear and greed have far more pronounced effects. A FOMO spike in call IV or a panic that implies an unrealistic probability of bankruptcy are both real, recurring opportunities.
Rent versus flipping houses

Risk premia are commercial real estate. You collect rent. You know there will be vacancies, repairs, defaulting tenants — losing trades. But across the portfolio and across time, structural demand means the rents outweigh the costs. That is a durable business.

Inefficiencies are flipping distressed houses at auction. Find a $500,000 house for $250,000 and you make a fortune on that single deal. But you have no idea when the next one appears. Wonderful when it happens; impossible to build a business plan around. Build your core portfolio on risk premia and take the house flips opportunistically — never rely on them.

What the rigorous sources say about all this

Almost everything in this guide comes from people who sell options for a living or teach it on YouTube. It is worth hearing from the quantitative side, because they are markedly less enthusiastic — and they are probably right.

Theta is not income

Benn Eifert (QVR) decomposes an option position and shows theta is compensation for gamma, vanna and volga risk — payment for carrying exposures, not a yield. Worse: he argues the genuine risk-premium slice is often smaller than retail transaction costs. If true, a large share of retail premium selling is paying the spread to collect a premium that no longer exists by the time you have paid to get in and out.

The premium is thinner than it looks

Falkenblog puts the raw variance risk premium at roughly 1–2 volatility points. Not nothing, but not the bonanza implied by "IV is always overpriced." The same analysis notes that VXX's famous decay — 97.8% measured to about 2012 — is mostly structural contango, not risk-premium capture — a distinction constantly blurred in retail content.

A backtest that failed replication

tastytrade's own "Straddle Stops" SPY study produced a lot different results when an independent trader rebuilt it using real bid-ask data rather than mid prices. This is a caution about every backtest number in this guide, including the ones I find persuasive. Mid-price backtests flatter strategies that cross the spread often.

How retail actually loses

The MIT-hosted de Silva, So and Smith paper reports retail losses of 5–9% on average and 10–14% for high expected-volatility earnings announcements.

What I take from this, honestly

The variance risk premium is real and documented. But it is thin, uneven and easily eaten by costs — and the people with the least favourable transaction costs are exactly the retail traders being told it is a reliable income stream.

That does not mean do not trade. It means: trade liquid products where the spread is small, do not overtrade, and be sceptical of any backtest quoted at mid price. If a strategy's edge is 1–2 volatility points and your round trip costs more than that, you have no strategy — you have a hobby with a subscription fee.

Where a small trader may look for evidence

A small account does not create an edge. Highly traded markets are competitive, and less-traded markets can have worse spreads, smaller displayed size and harder exits. Treat every claimed edge as a hypothesis that needs evidence after realistic costs.

These are research areas, not advantages you can assume:

Longer time horizons

A longer holding period can reduce trading frequency, but it does not remove professional competition or prove an edge.

Costs eat edge for breakfast

In a classroom a trade is "buy at $1.00, sell at $1.10, make 10¢." In reality:

  • Visible costs: commissions and exchange fees.
  • Hidden costs: the bid-ask spread and slippage — the difference between the quoted price and the price you actually get.

Trade an option with a 10¢ spread and you effectively start the trade about 10% down. You can usually get filled somewhere near the middle, but on a mark-to-market basis you show a loss the moment you enter, because guaranteeing an immediate exit means hitting the bid or lifting the ask. Your edge has to be big enough to clear that friction before it is an edge at all.

Broken-wing butterfly INTERMEDIATE

A butterfly with one wing further out than the other. Often opens for a credit, and can have no risk on one side.

The catch: the "you cannot lose more than you paid" rule for butterflies only holds when the wings are equidistant. Break that and you can lose more.

Calendar / diagonal INTERMEDIATE

Sell a near-dated option, buy a longer-dated one at the same strike (calendar) or a different strike (diagonal). You are trading forward volatility, not direction.

The catch: a calendar is not a simple long-volatility trade, and its risk is not obvious from a payoff diagram.

The 112 — a case study in why win rate lies

The "112" buys a put debit spread funded by selling two extra far out-of-the-money puts for an overall net credit. A five-year record of 109 trades looks superb: 88% win rate, +$134,000 net, profit factor 2.6.

Now the rest of it. Average win $2,253. Average loss $6,200 — nearly three times bigger. Total losses about $81,000 across 13 losing trades, and a maximum drawdown just under $30,000. And on one occasion a 305% VIX spike triggered stops without the S&P even falling 10% — the danger was not a crash, just a jump in fear.

That is the whole lesson of Level 06 in a single strategy. An 88% win rate tells you almost nothing on its own.

Two traps

1. "You cannot lose more than you paid" is only true if the wings are equidistant from the body. Move one wing and you have a broken-wing butterfly — a different structure with genuinely different risk, and you can lose more than the entry price.

2. A call butterfly and a put butterfly at the same strikes are economically identical — same max profit, max loss and break-evens. Choose whichever keeps the body out of the money, because out-of-the-money options are more liquid.

Volatility view: buy butterflies when IV is high, because they are cheap then, and you profit as IV falls. A pin is far more likely in a calm market than a wild one.

"Isn't that double the risk?"

At expiry, only one side can finish in the money. That does not limit the position's open risk. Before expiry, mark-to-market value uses current option prices. A volatility spike can raise both legs at once, create a loss on both marks, and increase margin before the underlying reaches either strike.

Long straddle / strangle ADVANCED. Buy instead of sell and every sign flips: a long straddle is a V-shape, a pure bet on a big move in either direction. Large positive gamma, large positive vega, and a large negative theta you pay every day for the privilege. You want the move to happen fast.

Reading the delta of a long straddle tells you what you want next: as the price moves down away from the strike, delta becomes more negative — the position is telling you it wants more downside. Move up, and delta turns positive. The sign of delta always names the direction that helps you.

Margin reality

Short straddles and strangles have unlimited loss above the call strike and require large margin. In the worked example in Level 05, a four-lot GME straddle consumed ~$25,000 of margin while the equivalent iron condor consumed ~$3,000. Same directional view, 8× the capital. That difference often matters more than the small edge you give up by buying wings.

$10,000 account: no — unlimited loss and heavy margin. Trade the iron condor instead

Widen the gap between the strikes as far as you can while still collecting a net credit. A wider gap means a wider zone where you can hit the home run. Put–call parity & synthetics

One equation that dissolves half the strategy names you have just learned.

Put–call parity (European, same strike and expiry) C − P = S − PV(K)
C = call price · P = put price · S = spot · PV(K) = present value of the strike

Rearrange for the stock and you get S = C − P + PV(K): long a call, short a put, plus cash, is synthetic long stock. The options give the directional payoff; the cash component is the financing leg — the amount that, invested at today's rate, grows into the strike by expiry. That financing term is why a synthetic's instantaneous P/L sits above its expiry P/L before expiry.

The middle row is worth dwelling on. A call debit spread and a put credit spread with the same strikes have the same expiry payoff. One opens for a debit and one for a credit — that is a presentation difference, not an economic one. Pick the one with the tighter spread and better margin.

The bottom row is the one that changes behaviour. Someone who says "I would never sell a put, it is far too risky, but I am happy selling covered calls" is holding two identical risk positions and believing they are different.

Option-specific risks

The Greeks cover the smooth, known sensitivities. These five risks are event-driven or structural, and they show up exactly when things are already going badly.

Pin risk

The stock closes almost exactly at your short strike on expiry day. You cannot know whether you will be assigned, and you carry an unknown 100-share position over the weekend.

Fix: close short options trading near their strike before the close. Or trade cash-settled index options, which have no shares to deliver.

Early exercise risk

American options can be exercised against you at any time. Rational triggers: a deep ITM put where the interest on the strike exceeds the remaining extrinsic value, or a deep ITM call ahead of a dividend larger than its extrinsic value.

Fix: watch extrinsic value on your short ITM options and know your ex-dividend dates.

Liquidity risk

The screen price can be an illusion. A $2.10 bid against a $3.80 ask has a mid of $2.95, but exiting at the $2.10 bid surrenders ~29% of its midpoint value to slippage immediately. In stress, market makers pull quotes and liquidity vanishes entirely.

Fix: check open interest before volume. Avoid thin chains entirely — never rely on holding them to expiry to dodge exit costs.

Gap risk

The price jumps from one level to another with no trading in between — overnight, on earnings, on news. This breaks delta hedging completely: your hedge was right at Friday's close and catastrophically wrong at Monday's open, and you could not adjust it.

Fix: size short-gamma positions so a worst-case gap is survivable, and prefer defined-risk structures. Own the risk instead of pretending you can continuously hedge it.

Borrow cost risk

Hedging often needs short stock, which needs borrowable shares. If a name becomes hard to borrow, the fee spikes; in the extreme you face a mandatory recall and are forced to buy back in the open market. A trade that looked attractive on paper can turn poor once borrow costs are included. Fix: check borrow availability and fees before entering anything that may need a short-stock hedge, and prefer structures that do not.

The professional standard

Gaps are known to be possible. A trader who blows up on something they knew could happen is not unlucky — they are gambling. Position sizing is the defence, not cleverness after the fact.

Glossary

Assignment
Being selected to fulfil the obligation of an option you sold. The OCC randomly assigns clearing firms. Your broker then uses an approved method, which may differ, to select a short client. Settlement is at the strike.
Backwardation
A term structure where short-dated implied volatility exceeds long-dated. A signal of acute stress or a known imminent event such as earnings. Inherently unstable; usually reverts to contango.
Break-even
The underlying price at which a position makes exactly zero at expiry. Strike + premium for a long call; strike − premium for a long put.
Contango
A term structure where long-dated implied volatility exceeds short-dated. The normal state, and one of the better predictors of short-volatility returns.
Delta
Change in option price for a $1 change in the underlying. Also serves as the equivalent stock position, the hedge ratio, and a rough proxy for probability of finishing in the money.
Delta hedging
Trading the underlying to keep net delta near zero at a point in time. The result still depends on the hedge path, jumps, changing Greeks and transaction costs.
Extrinsic value
The part of an option's premium above its intrinsic value. Driven by time remaining and implied volatility. Decays to zero at expiry.
Gamma
Rate of change of delta. Positive when long options, negative when short. Peaks at the money and explodes near expiry.
Gamma scalping
Delta rebalancing of a long-gamma position. A favourable path can help, but jumps, discrete hedging and costs can outweigh it.
Implied volatility (IV)
The volatility input that reproduces an observed option price in a chosen model. It is useful for comparing prices, not an unbiased forecast.
Intrinsic value
What an option would be worth if exercised immediately. max(0, S − K) for a call, max(0, K − S) for a put. Never negative.
Leverage effect
The leading explanation for negative spot-vol correlation in equities: as a stock falls, debt stays fixed, so the debt-to-equity ratio rises and the business becomes genuinely riskier and more volatile.
Moneyness
Where the strike sits relative to spot. Calls are ITM when the strike is below spot; puts are ITM when the strike is above spot. ATM options carry the most extrinsic value and the largest gamma, theta and vega.
Multiplier
Shares controlled per contract — almost always 100 for US equities and ETFs. Premiums are quoted per share, so a $2.33 quote costs $233.
Open interest
Total contracts currently outstanding. It is one clue, not a liquidity guarantee. Check the live bid-ask width, displayed size, recent trading and the ability to close both legs.
Pin risk
The uncertainty faced when the underlying closes almost exactly at a short strike on expiry day. You cannot know whether you will be assigned.
Put–call parity
A no-dividend European relation: C − P = S − PV(K). Dividends, financing, exercise style and discrete cash payments must be included before comparing positions such as a covered call and a short put.
Realized volatility (RV)
The volatility a stock actually experienced over a past window. Backward-looking, and still an estimate — the answer depends on lookback and estimator.
Rho
Sensitivity to a 1-point change in the risk-free rate. Higher rates raise calls and lower puts. Negligible for most short-dated strategies.
Rule of 16
√252 ≈ 16, so annualised IV ÷ 16 gives the expected daily move. IV 32% → ±2% a day.
Skew
How implied volatility varies across strikes at one expiry. Equities show a downward-sloping smirk: OTM puts imply higher volatility than ATM, which implies more than OTM calls.
Structure vs strategy
A structure is a payoff shape with a Greek signature — a tool. A strategy requires an edge: a reasoned view differing from what the market has priced. Structures never supply edge.
Theta
Expected daily loss of value from time passing. The rent paid to be long gamma, or collected for being short it. Not a source of edge.
Variance risk premium (VRP)
The persistent tendency for implied volatility to exceed the realized volatility that follows. Compensation to sellers for carrying convex, gap-exposed risk. Not present on every name or at every time.
Vega
Change in option price for a 1-percentage-point change in implied volatility. Highest for at-the-money, long-dated options.
Volatility surface
Implied volatility plotted across both strike and expiry. Decomposes into skew (across strikes) and term structure (across expiries).
Yang–Zhang estimator
An OHLC estimator that includes an overnight component. It can be useful, but its result still depends on assumptions, data quality and sampling choices.

Where sources disagree

Rather than smooth these over, the guide prints them. The main ones:

  • High-IV entries: both the 71,417-trade condor study and the 41,600-trade short-put study found high IV more profitable. A third study (condors versus strangles) found the same, but only once a stop rule was attached — without one, the high-IV drawdowns were severe.
  • Strike selection: systematic sources use delta; one experienced trader calls that "a bad way to pick your strike prices" and uses chart support.
  • Is selling premium an edge? The YouTube channels say broadly yes. Eifert and Falkenblog say the premium is thin — 1–2 volatility points — and can be smaller than a retail trader's costs. The guide sides with the sceptics while still teaching the strategies.
  • Early-close triggers: "10–20% of time value left" and "80–90% of profit" are not the same rule for a deep in-the-money option.
